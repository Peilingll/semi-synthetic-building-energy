\subsection{Dataset Statistics}
\label{sec:dataset_statistics}

The \ac{SVI} manifest described in \cref{sec:svi_acquisition_filtering} contains 10,104 buildings and 47,238 images.
After linkage with the quality-controlled and TABULA-NL-matched building-level reference table, 88 images associated with 18 buildings were excluded from model development and evaluation because of invalid 3DBAG geometry or unsuccessful archetype matching.

The counts of 10,104 and 10,086 do not refer to the same data stage: the former represents the \ac{SVI} delivery set after image curation, whereas the latter is the modelling sample with complete experimental inputs and reference targets.

The experimental sample is highly concentrated geographically: Amsterdam contains 8,011 buildings, corresponding to 79.4\% of the dataset.
Its building-type composition also differs substantially from that of the eligible four-city registry reference pool, which contains 124,784 residential buildings with EPC records and valid TABULA-NL matches.
Apartment Blocks (AB) account for approximately 88\% of the experimental sample, compared with approximately 54\% of the reference pool.
The energy-class distributions also differ, but the difference cannot be characterised simply as a shift towards better-performing buildings in the experimental sample.
Under the binary grouping used in this study, buildings in classes A to C account for 70.3\% of the experimental sample and 76.5\% of the reference pool.
Subsequent results should therefore be interpreted as estimates for the \ac{SVI}-covered sample rather than as unbiased estimates for the residential building stock of the four cities.

This mismatch in sample composition is consistent with the contributor-driven, non-random spatial coverage of Mapillary and is also affected by the study-area boundaries, residential eligibility criteria, and image-quality filtering.
The observed mismatch is not attributed further to specific contributor behaviour.
The effect of sample composition on model performance is evaluated in \cref{sec:sample_composition_effects}, and the resulting limitation on the scope of the study is discussed in \cref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Route Families and Evaluation Settings}
\label{sec:route_families}

\textbf{Route families.}
Four evaluation routes distinguish a no-information baseline, reference-attribute inference, direct prediction from imagery, and attribute-mediated inference.
The M identifiers denote inference routes rather than different datasets or downstream-model families.

M0 is a \textbf{uniform random-guessing baseline} that assigns equal probability to every class in the seven-class or binary label space.
Metrics are reported as analytic expectations, including a seven-class accuracy of $1/7$ and a binary macro recall of 0.5, so the reported values do not depend on a random seed.
The prediction rule uses neither the reference labels nor the class prevalence, although analytic expectations for prevalence-dependent metrics are calculated from the evaluation-label frequencies.
A separate \textbf{majority guess}, which always predicts the most frequent class, is reported where needed to expose inflation caused by class imbalance.
Majority guessing alone yields a $\pm$1 accuracy of 0.612 and a binary accuracy of 0.702; neither metric is therefore interpreted in isolation.

The M1 and M3 attribution comparison uses a shared fitted-model protocol.
Separate LightGBM classifiers are trained for the binary and seven-class tasks.
In the pooled setting, each classifier is fitted once using reference-derived features from the complete development set.
The same fitted parameters then receive the M1 reference features and each set of M3 vision-derived features.
In the Amsterdam-held-out setting, each classifier is refitted once using reference features from the source cities and is again shared by M1 and M3 within that setting.
The M3 minus M1 difference therefore excludes the effect of downstream retraining and reflects the combined consequences of replacing the three upstream attributes, changing the resulting TABULA-NL cell, and retrieving different U-values.

\textbf{Evaluation settings.}
The primary evaluation uses the fixed building-level split defined in \cref{sec:data_partitioning}: 8,068 development buildings and a fixed hold-out set of 2,018 buildings.
The five development folds support model selection and out-of-fold (OOF) analyses, whereas all pooled headline results are reported on the untouched hold-out set.
A separate Amsterdam-held-out transfer evaluation trains and selects all trainable components using 2,075 buildings from Rotterdam, Utrecht, and Delft and evaluates them on 8,011 Amsterdam buildings.
The original pooled split is not retained in this setting.
Because Amsterdam is the only held-out city, this experiment provides evidence for transfer to one target city rather than an estimate of average cross-city generalisability.

The ResNet-50, DINOv2, and InternVL3-2B configurations retain the parameter-update scopes, image counts, and multi-image aggregation procedures defined in \cref{sec:vision_inference_strategies} and \cref{tab:vision_configs}.
Each configuration produces a corresponding M3 attribute-mediated variant and a separately configured M2 direct energy-class comparator.
Their training procedures are not repeated here.

\subsection{Prediction Tasks and Metrics}
\label{sec:prediction_tasks_metrics}

\textbf{Upstream metrics.}
Construction-year prediction is evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE), $R^2$, and TABULA period accuracy.
Because the TABULA periods have unequal widths, MAE alone does not indicate whether a prediction crosses a period boundary.
The $R^2$ score compares prediction error with the variation in construction year within the evaluation set and therefore helps identify cases in which a low MAE partly reflects a narrow target distribution.
Building-type prediction is evaluated primarily using macro-F1, with accuracy reported as a supplementary metric.
A large difference between these metrics indicates sensitivity to class imbalance; $R^2$ is not applicable to this categorical task.
Floor-count prediction is evaluated using MAE and $R^2$.

\textbf{Archetype-cell metrics.}
TABULA uses the joint archetype cell rather than either attribute in isolation.
Joint accuracy is therefore defined as the proportion of buildings for which both size class and construction period are correct:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:joint_acc}
	\mathrm{JointAcc}
	= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
	\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{s}_i = s_i \;\land\; p(\hat{y}_i) = p(y_i)\right].
\end{equation}

Uniform random guessing across the 24 cells has an expected joint accuracy of $1/24 \approx 0.042$.
Macro-cell recall, defined as the unweighted mean recall across occupied cells, is also reported to reveal performance outside the dominant cells.
Because the evaluation population is highly concentrated, a majority-cell guess already achieves a joint accuracy of 0.790 in the pooled hold-out set and 0.868 in the Amsterdam population.
Joint accuracy is therefore not interpreted without these baselines.

\textbf{Downstream tasks.}
The energy-class target is evaluated at two levels of granularity.
The primary task is binary classification of A to C versus D to G.
This grouping corresponds to a threshold of 250\,kWh/(m$^2\cdot$year), the upper boundary of class C, and follows the coarse grouping used by \textcite{sun2026}.
The experimental population contains 70.3\% of buildings in classes A to C and 29.7\% in classes D to G.
The seven-class A to G task is retained as a diagnostic evaluation of seven-class separability and the limitations of the available predictors (\cref{sec:sample_composition_effects}).
The binary classifier is trained independently with a binary objective; its outputs are not obtained by collapsing predictions from the seven-class model after inference.

\textbf{Downstream metrics.}
The seven-class task reports macro-F1, exact accuracy, and $\pm$1 accuracy.
Macro-F1 gives equal weight to each energy class, exact accuracy supports comparison with studies that report accuracy, including \textcite{hettinga2023}, and $\pm$1 accuracy measures whether the predicted class lies within one ordinal class of the reference class.
M0 is reported as a lower reference point.
However, majority guessing alone yields a $\pm$1 accuracy of 0.612, compared with approximately 0.39 under uniform random guessing.
The $\pm$1 metric is therefore reported only alongside macro-F1 and the relevant baselines.

The binary task reports macro precision, macro recall, and macro-F1 at the default probability threshold of 0.5, consistent with the macro-averaged reporting used in the comparison literature.
Aggregate metrics can conceal asymmetric predictions, so class-specific precision and recall, particularly recall for classes D to G, are also examined.
Binary accuracy is likewise interpreted against the majority guess, which reaches 0.702 by assigning every building to classes A to C.
The principal LightGBM protocol uses no class weighting, avoiding a change in the effective class prior and maintaining a common configuration across tasks.
The class-balanced sensitivity variant increases macro-F1 by 0.071 and is documented in Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix_a}.

\textbf{Error attribution.}
Downstream errors in M3 are separated according to whether the required upstream attributes are correct.
Cases in which the attributes are correct but the energy-class prediction remains incorrect are assigned to the downstream task, whereas cases containing an upstream attribute error are assigned to visual extraction.
This decomposition and the performance difference between M1 and M3 provide complementary views of the downstream cost of attribute substitution.

\textbf{Metric definitions.}
Let $N$ denote the number of evaluated buildings, $y_i$ the reference value, $\hat{y}_i$ the prediction, and $K$ the number of classes:

\begin{itemize}
	\item $\mathrm{Accuracy} = N^{-1}\sum_i \mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_i = y_i]$.
	\item For each class $c$, precision is the proportion of predictions assigned to $c$ that are correct, recall is the proportion of reference observations in $c$ that are recovered, and $F1_c = 2P_cR_c/(P_c+R_c)$.
	\item Macro precision, macro recall, and macro-F1 are the unweighted means of the corresponding class-level values, with weight $1/K$ assigned to each class rather than to each building.
	\item $\mathrm{MAE} = N^{-1}\sum_i |\hat{y}_i - y_i|$.
	\item $R^2 = 1 - \sum_i(\hat{y}_i-y_i)^2 / \sum_i(y_i-\bar{y})^2$, where $\bar{y}$ is the mean reference value. A value of zero is equivalent to predicting the reference mean, and a negative value indicates poorer performance.
	\item $\mathrm{PeriodAcc} = N^{-1}\sum_i \mathbf{1}[p(\hat{y}_i) = p(y_i)]$, where $p(\cdot)$ maps a construction year to a TABULA period.
	\item $\mathrm{Acc}_{\pm 1} = N^{-1}\sum_i \mathbf{1}[|r(\hat{y}_i)-r(y_i)| \leq 1]$, where $r(\cdot)$ gives the ordinal rank of an energy class from A to G.
	\item Macro-cell recall is the unweighted mean recall across occupied TABULA-NL cells.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Physically Weighted Archetype-Difference Metric}
\label{sec:weighted_archetype_metric}

\textbf{Physically weighted archetype-difference measure.}
Cell-level metrics record only whether an assignment is correct.
They therefore assign the same error to confusion between adjacent cells with similar U-values and to confusion between distant cells whose U-values differ substantially.
To account for this difference, the transmission heat-transfer coefficient $H'_{tr}$ is calculated for each building using the TABULA U-values of the assigned cell and reconstructed 3DBAG envelope areas.
Shared walls are treated as adiabatic, and the window-to-wall ratio (WWR) is the only assumed input parameter.
Values calculated from predicted cells are compared with values calculated from reference cells using building-level MAE and $R^2$, together with a floor-area-weighted stock-level heat-loss bias.
The stock-level measure retains systematic error while allowing building-level errors with opposite signs to offset one another.
This analysis is a physically weighted comparison of predicted and reference archetype assignments; it is not a validation against measured energy consumption.

For building $i$, the transmission heat-transfer coefficient is

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:htr}
	\begin{split}
		H'_{tr,i} = \frac{1}{A_{floor,i}} \Bigl[
		 & U_{wall,i}A_{wall,i}(1-\mathrm{WWR}) + U_{window,i}A_{wall,i}\,\mathrm{WWR}                     \\
		 & + U_{roof,i}A_{roof,i} + U_{floor,i}A_{ground,i} \Bigr], \qquad [\mathrm{W/(K\,m^2)}] .
	\end{split}
\end{equation}

The corresponding coefficient of determination is

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:htr_r2}
	R^2_{H'_{tr}}
	= 1 - \frac{\sum_i\left(H'_{tr,\mathrm{pred},i} - H'_{tr,\mathrm{ref},i}\right)^2}
	{\sum_i\left(H'_{tr,\mathrm{ref},i} - \overline{H'_{tr,\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^2},
\end{equation}

and the signed stock-level bias is

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:bias_stock}
	\mathrm{Bias}_{stock}
	= \frac{\sum_i H'_{tr,\mathrm{pred},i}A_{floor,i} - \sum_i H'_{tr,\mathrm{ref},i}A_{floor,i}}
	{\sum_i H'_{tr,\mathrm{ref},i}A_{floor,i}} .
\end{equation}

\Cref{eq:htr} is evaluated twice for each building: once using the U-values retrieved from the reference cell and once using those retrieved from the predicted cell.
Both calculations use the same reconstructed areas and WWR.
A correctly assigned cell therefore produces zero error.
\Cref{eq:htr_r2} expresses agreement with the variation in the reference-derived coefficients, while \cref{eq:bias_stock} retains the sign of aggregate error; a value of zero indicates no net stock-level bias.

\textbf{WWR assumption and sensitivity analysis.}
The 3DBAG geometry does not distinguish window openings within the facade, while window U-values are substantially higher than wall U-values for the same period.
For example, the NL.01 values are 5.2 and 1.61\,$\mathrm{W/(m^2K)}$, respectively (\cref{tab:tabula_uvalues}).
The baseline assumption of $\mathrm{WWR} = 0.25$ assigns 25\% of the facade area to windows.
Because WWR is the only assumed parameter in \cref{eq:htr}, a local one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis was conducted, following a standard approach in building-performance analysis \parencite{pang2020}.
Recalculating \cref{tab:htr_metrics} with WWR values of 0.15 and 0.35 did not change the ranking of the three vision configurations; the complete results are reported in \cref{tab:wwr_sensitivity}.
The reference-derived and prediction-derived calculations use the same WWR and reconstructed areas, so their difference arises solely from the U-values associated with the reference and predicted archetype cells.

\subsection{Upstream Attribute Extraction}
\label{sec:upstream_attribute_extraction}

The three vision configurations produced a consistent overall ranking on the fixed hold-out set (\cref{tab:upstream_attributes}; metric definitions are provided in \cref{sec:prediction_tasks_metrics}).
DINOv2 achieved the best result in six of the seven reported metric columns: building-type macro-F1 (0.520), construction-year MAE (9.45 years), construction-year $R^2$ (0.679), period accuracy (0.901), floor-count MAE (0.377), and floor-count $R^2$ (0.569).
ResNet-50 achieved a slightly higher building-type accuracy than DINOv2 (0.915 versus 0.901), but a lower macro-F1 (0.492 versus 0.520).
InternVL3-2B produced the lowest value for every reported metric.

Construction year showed the strongest building-level predictive performance among the three attributes.

Because the studies used different datasets and label sources, this comparison indicates only the approximate performance range and is not a direct benchmark.
In contrast, zero-shot InternVL3-2B obtained a negative construction-year $R^2$ of $-0.543$.
Its outputs were concentrated at a small set of years, including 1850, 1900, 1950, 1970, and 1980, and did not reproduce the building-level variation in the reference years (\cref{fig:year_floor_scatter}).

The low MAE therefore partly reflects the restricted target range.
The corresponding $R^2$ values of 0.569 and 0.551 show that both trained configurations captured building-level variation beyond a mean prediction, but the result is bounded by the narrow floor-count distribution of the evaluated stock.

\subsection{Joint-Cell Assignment}
\label{sec:joint_cell_assignment}

Joint-cell assignment results are reported in \cref{tab:joint_cell}.
DINOv2 and ResNet-50 achieved almost identical joint accuracies of 0.825 and 0.826, respectively, compared with an expected accuracy of 0.042 under uniform random assignment to the 24 cells.
However, 1,595 of the 2,018 hold-out buildings belonged to the modal AB\,\textbar\,NL.01 cell (\cref{fig:cell_recall}).
Assigning every building to this cell would achieve an accuracy of 0.790, leaving gains of only 3.5 and 3.6 percentage points for DINOv2 and ResNet-50.
InternVL3-2B achieved a joint accuracy of 0.342, below the majority-cell baseline.

Macro-cell recall shows that performance was unevenly distributed across the occupied cells.
DINOv2 achieved recalls of 0.93 for AB\,\textbar\,NL.01 and 0.79 for TH\,\textbar\,NL.01, whereas recall was generally no higher than 0.25 for cells after 1964 and was zero for all MFH cells (\cref{fig:cell_recall}).
Its unweighted recall across the 21 occupied cells was 0.180, compared with 0.048 for a classifier that always assigns the majority cell.
InternVL3-2B achieved a similar macro-cell recall of 0.168 through a different prediction pattern: its assignments were dispersed across more cells and included correct predictions for cells with very few observations, such as SFH\,\textbar\,NL.02 ($n = 1$).
Because macro-cell recall gives each occupied cell equal weight, this value is sensitive to such sparse cells and does not indicate stable coverage of the TABULA matrix.

\subsection{Physically Weighted Archetype-Difference Scoring}
\label{sec:weighted_archetype_scoring}

Joint-cell metrics record whether an assignment is correct but do not distinguish errors by the thermal differences between cells.
\Cref{tab:htr_metrics} therefore reports the difference in $H'_{tr}$ defined in \cref{sec:weighted_archetype_metric}.
The separation between the trained and zero-shot configurations was larger on this scale than in joint accuracy.
DINOv2 and InternVL3-2B achieved joint accuracies of 0.825 and 0.342, respectively, while their building-level $H'_{tr}$ MAEs differed by a factor of 3.2 (0.150 versus 0.474\,$\mathrm{W/(K\,m^2)}$).
The larger error indicates that the zero-shot assignments also produced greater differences in the associated thermal transmittance values.
For example, the wall U-value decreases from 1.61\,$\mathrm{W/(m^2K)}$ in NL.01 to 0.27\,$\mathrm{W/(m^2K)}$ in NL.05 (\cref{tab:tabula_uvalues}).
The $H'_{tr}$ $R^2$ values were 0.806 for DINOv2, 0.771 for ResNet-50, and 0.439 for InternVL3-2B.
Because this comparison does not use energy classes, it is independent of the building-level class consistency examined in \cref{sec:building_level_consistency}.

\subsection{Cross-City Transfer to Amsterdam}
\label{sec:cross_city_transfer}

The transfer evaluation instead trained and selected all trainable components using 2,075 buildings from Rotterdam, Utrecht, and Delft and evaluated them on all 8,011 Amsterdam buildings.
No Amsterdam building or energy class was used for model fitting or selection.
This setting approximates deployment to a target city without labelled training data.
Because Amsterdam was the only city held out, the experiment tests transfer to Amsterdam but does not estimate average cross-city generalisability.

\section{Diagnostic Analyses}
\label{sec:diagnostic_analyses}

The route-level comparisons establish the overall effect of replacing reference attributes with vision-derived inputs, but they do not identify which attributes account for the observed differences or how the composition and definition of the evaluation target constrain performance.
The following analyses therefore examine three factors: the downstream contribution and source suitability of individual attributes, the consistency of energy classes at building level, and the effect of sample composition on seven-class classification.

\subsection{Downstream Feature Utility and Source Suitability}
\label{sec:feature_utility}

\Cref{tab:feature_ablation} evaluates the marginal contribution of the existing downstream attributes and the additional contribution of reconstructed geometry.
Among the attributes already used by the reference route, construction year produced the largest performance reduction when removed: macro-F1 decreased by 0.032 in the seven-class task and by 0.030 in the binary task.
Removing floor count produced smaller reductions of 0.012 and 0.011, while building type and city had limited marginal effects under the full feature configuration.

Removing the four TABULA-derived U-values produced virtually no change in either task.
This result reflects conditional redundancy rather than a general lack of relevance of thermal properties: once building type and construction period are included, the tabulated U-values are deterministic functions of those attributes and do not provide independent building-level information.
