ABSTRACT
In the European Union (EU), the built environment is responsible for 40% of energy consumption. To reduce this energy consumption, policymakers require insight into current efficiency of the building stock and potential for improvement. The EU Energy Performance of Buildings Directive provides guidelines for surveying building performance and assigning energy labels; however, implementation has been slow. To address the backlog, assigning labels based on modelling rather than individual surveying might be an attractive, quicker alternative. The EU TABULA building typologies is such a model; however, in our validation against 2.5 million houses surveyed individually already, the instrument correctly classifies only 26% of buildings. This study proposes that machine learning can help leverage the abundance of (spatial) open data to suggest energy labels more closely matching the quality of individual surveying. Various transparent machine learning algorithms were tested and optimized to select the most suitable technique to model energy labels. This study shows that a random forest classifier trained on open data can reach an accuracy of 71%, thereby demonstrating the potential of open data and machine learning to quickly generate better energy labels for an entire building stock.
1. Introduction
The rise in national and international climate agreements signed in recent years reflects the urgency of policymakers to reduce CO2-emissions. The largest of these is the 2015 Paris agreement [1], with many countries converting their participation into national pledges. Also, the European Union and its member states, have increased their 2020 targets to new 2030 targets [2] to translate their ratification of the Paris agreement into policy targets. To reduce energy consumption and CO2-emissions, policymakers consider a range of areas, including agriculture, transport, industry, or the built environment. The last of these can significantly contribute to the reduction of CO2-emissions [3]. In the European Union, for instance, the built environment is responsible for 40% of energy consumption [4]. The European Union has, therefore, issued an Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD) [5], with a wide range of guidelines for both new buildings and improvement of existing stock. As the number of new buildings each year constitutes approximately 1% of the existing stock [6], energy reduction of existing buildings is at least of equal importance to make an impact in reducing energy consumption of the built environment. Here, labelling is an important first step, as labels provide
insight into current efficiency and potential for improvement to homeowners and policymakers alike. In the European Union, this label is referred to as the Energy Performance Certificate (EPC). In other countries, such as the UK similar labels or certificates have been implemented, with the aim of benchmarking buildings and move citizens to retrofitting of these buildings [7]. While the European Union member states have already implemented EPBD legislation in 2009 [8], many buildings are still without an EPC. For example, in Norway, which implemented the EPBD in 2010, only 20% had an EPC by the end of 2016 [9]. In the Netherlands, only 30% of buildings had an EPC in 2017 [10]. To address this backlog in assessed buildings, assigning labels based on modelling, rather than on individual surveying, could be an attractive, much quicker option. The European Union has therefore developed a building typology to help policymakers, called TABULA (Typology Approach for Building Stock Energy Assessment). For residential buildings, the TABULA typology suggests an energy label based on building type, building year, country, and climate zone [11]. As a European-wide typology, it makes energy performance comparable between countries and their different building traditions [12]. However, this instrument is only effective if it accurately predicts real energy performance, i.e., if it corresponds to an assessment

2. Background
In this section, the relevant background information on energy labels and energy label modelling is provided. The value and goals of energy labels, and the strategy for determining the label are established. Furthermore, relevant AI concepts and techniques are explained. This section leads to the methodology proposed in this paper.
2.1. The energy label
The EPBD defines a wide range of measures on the energy performance of buildings. This directive, first implemented in 2010, provides guidelines for designing new buildings, mapping performance of existing buildings, and inspecting heating and cooling installations [24]. To evaluate energy performance of existing buildings, there are three methods [13]: 1) benchmarking, 2) rating, and 3) labelling. The European Union as well as several other countries, have selected labelling as their evaluation methodology, to create insight into the performance of the building, but also the possibilities of improvements [7].
In the European Union, labelling is done through the Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) [25]. The EPC considers the exposed parameter of the building, the insulation value of exposed surfaces, as well as installations in the building, such as air-conditioning, boilers, solar panels, and other heaters. EPC protocol describes how these elements should be assessed in an audit to derive an Energy Performance Index (EPI). For easier communication, EPI scores are translated into
letters (energy labels) from G to A++, where G is the poorest energy performance (see Table 1) [24]. EPC labelling has often been gradual. In the Netherlands, for instance, an EPC is required only when a building is sold or when the lease of a rental building is renewed. While owners can also apply for an energy label voluntarily, auditing is costly and therefore only likely after significant changes to the energy efficiency of their home. Housing cooperatives, for instance, may file for re-assessment after a large renovation, as a change in label may be a criterion for rent increase (Filippidou, 2017). After assessment, an EPC remains valid for ten years.
2.2. Modelling labels
Auditing individual buildings can vary in intensity. Ma et al. [26] distinguish three levels of intensity: “Level 1: walk through assessment, Level 2: energy survey and analysis, and Level 3: detailed energy analysis”. However, even at the simplest level, a building-by-building assessment can take a long time to supply policymakers with a complete overview of energy performance of the building stock. The first data-driven energy label tool, developed by Poel et al. [27] and based on the protocol described in the European Union directive of 2007 [25], still requires input from an on-site auditor to calculate the label. Later, Ma et al. [26] provide an overview of the different technologies and methodologies devised to model energy labels in different countries. The most recent of the methodologies described by Ma et al. [26] use the TABULA [11] building typologies to model the energy label, at least in part. Furthermore, they explore how these labels can be used for decision-making in retrofitting. Florio and Teissier [28] also use the TABULA [11] building typologies in combination with a French database to model the energy labels in France.
2.3. Modelling alternatives: machine learning
As proposed by Ahmad [29]; data science techniques can offer new options for energy label modelling. An increasingly popular data science technique is the use of AI which has been successfully applied by Khayatian et al. [30]. In this study, the authors have applied neural networks with a highly detailed dataset of building properties to predict the energy label of individual buildings. Their study reports an accuracy of 95% falling within ±3 confidence intervals of the heat demand indicator measured in kWh/m2. However, for their training of the algorithm they use at least some of the detailed features used to directly determine the energy label—available only for buildings that have already applied for an EPC. Validation of current model (TABULA)
One of the methodologies currently used to determine the energy labels of buildings that are not officially certified yet, is to use TABULA. In this paper, this method is validated to assess the baseline modelling quality. This section explains the data used to model the TABULA labels and shows the results.
2.4. Benchmark data
This study starts with testing the TABULA model [11] for the Netherlands, as required input is readily available as open data, as is an extensive database of certified energy labels against which to test. In Section 4, the performance of several AI models will be tested against the same database. This database of EPCs is maintained by the RVO government agency that supports, amongst others, sustainable entrepreneurship [10]. At time of acquisition, this database contained 2,574,227 EPCs. The Netherlands contains approximately 8.8 million buildings, of which 7.7 million are residential. The database therefore contains approximately 30% of buildings, leaving nearly 70% still in need of a label. The quality of energy labels was validated by the Dutch Court of Auditors [31]. From a relatively small sample, this agency has identified that 86% of labels were correctly assigned. Of the remaining 14%, half
had been assigned one label better than was found in the audit, and the other half had been assigned one label worse. This means that the ground truth quality of the validation data is 86%; that there is no unbiased, gold standard available in sufficient quantities; and that, for the Netherlands, 86% model accuracy is likely to be a hard ceiling. While Hardy and Glew [32] found in their study that the error of the UK EPC database was higher, this might have been due to different certification strategies. Ground truth quality is generally relevant information for a model validation study. However, when one uses AI techniques to model, it is crucial to have this information as the quality of model results is dependent on the quality of both input data and test data. Another important assumption is that the models need to perform at a higher level than the “majority vote”. The “majority vote” is the accuracy that would be achieved when all buildings are assigned the label of the highest occurring label. Fig. 1 shows that this is the label C, with a majority vote of 33%. The models validated and designed in this study should have an accuracy greater than 33% to outperform such a “majority vote”=.
2.5. TABULA data for the Netherlands
The TABULA model is based on the building typologies defined in the TABULA project [11]. In this project, every member state was commissioned to develop building typologies representative of a specific common type of household with universal energy performance properties. The countries were requested to define these typologies on three axes: building year, building type, and climate zone. The first, building year, is the most straightforward. Legislation on building homes has increased over the years leading to an increase in insulation and other energy requirements; and building year is, therefore, likely indicative of energy performance. In this study, the building year can be directly extracted from the BAG, the Dutch Registry for Addresses and Buildings [33]. The second, building type, has been selected as it is an indicator of the number of walls a building shares with another building. When a building shares walls it is better insulated from the cold in winter, or the warmth in summer. Therefore, a detached house sharing no walls requires more insulation than an apartment with shared walls. The data in this study related to building type were generated by doing an analysis of shared walls, using the building polygons from the BAG and the methodology of Hettinga et al. [34]. Finally, the third parameter is climate zone. In larger countries, the outdoor temperature profile can vary greatly between the north-south or east-west poles of a country; and this variance can influence the energy requirements and construction of individual buildings. 4. Exploring machine learning alternatives
This section describes and compares the performance of several machine learning algorithms to model energy labels. With the selection of the algorithm, a variation of hyperparameters (parameters than can be used to adapt the learning process of the algorithm) of these algorithms is also tested, to ensure that the model is tuned to the research questions, as well as to the boundary conditions for calculation (e.g., calculation time). The accuracy of these models is analyzed using a confusion matrix.
4.1. Machine learning models
There are various machine learning algorithms. Based on a range of principles they can produce diverse results while answering the same question with the same input data. Therefore, it is important to sample a variety of algorithms to find the one best suited to a specific study. Furthermore, algorithms have hyperparameters: settings to tune the learning strategy to the algorithms. These hyperparameters need to be explored to find not only the right algorithm, but also the correct learning process for each algorithm. In this study, four algorithms have been selected to model the energy labels with different combinations of hyperparameters to evaluate the most suitable combination to model the energy labels with the highest accuracy, given a calculation time of approximately 12 h on a standard business computer. The testing follows a protocol common in machine learning research. This means that 80% of the data used as input for the model will be used to train the model, 10% to validate the model (to tune the hyperparameters), and 10% to obtain the final accuracy data for the model. This third and final step is to ensure that the model that was trained is not only producing good results on data used to train the data, but also on new data. The results presented in this study are based on the third step, to ensure that the results are reproducible. For a comprehensive introduction to AI, This study refers to Bishop [35].

No direct input data was used that is normally required to calculate the official energy label, as done by, e.g., Khayatian et al. [30]. The model is designed to calculate the energy label for all residential buildings; and detailed information, such as heating system or window insulation value, is generally not available for all residential buildings. The algorithms tested in this study were implemented in scikit-learn [38], using the Python programming language.
4.3. Decision tree
The decision tree algorithm builds a logical model that bifurcates from a root node via several branches to, eventually, a final classification leaf node (Fig. 2). The number of branches, or depth of a decision tree, is set in the algorithm; it will affect calculation time and can affect result accuracy. To find an optimum, different depths must be explored. There
are several strategies to determine which decisions to select in the tree. The most common is to set the algorithm to use a cost function to analyze which of the splits represent the least loss of accuracy. Ultimately, when a building follows the tree to the last level, it will be assigned a specific energy label. For instance, it arrives at subset 1.2, navigates a few more levels down, and is finally determined to have an energy label of D. The process described here uses classification techniques to assign labels to buildings (labels are individual classes). However, in the database by RVO [10]; the energy indicator is also provided. To observe whether accuracy increases by using a regression algorithm (rather than a classification method) to see which label each building has obtained, the regression tree algorithm was tested, as well.
4.4. Random forest
The random forest algorithm is a more elaborate version of the decision tree algorithm. It is a group of trees (a forest) each trained on a subset of the training data and each generating its own predictions (Fig. 3). The decisions made by one tree (using the cost functions) frequently vary from the decisions made by another. This can lead to the same decision in the end, or to a different result. Using the majority vote principle, the result generated by the most trees is selected. The hyperparameters used in this algorithm are estimators, depth of tree, and percentage of training set. First, the number of estimators is set, meaning the number of trees is selected. For each study, the optimal number of trees needs to be determined, bearing in mind that adding more trees results in an increase in training time. To reduce training time, one can reduce the data in the training set. The training set, which is 80% of the full available dataset, can be reduced by selecting a random subset. One can for instance decide to only use 75% or 50% of the data; however, this can only be done if there is an abundance of training data. Finally, the depth of the tree, or number of branches, can be selected. Just as with the number of trees, the number of branches needs to be optimized as, beyond a certain depth, accuracy increases only marginally, while training time increases significantly. Experiments must be undertaken to find the optimal combination of hyperparameters that will result in both a reasonable calculation time (in this study 12 h on a regular computer) and accuracy. In Appendix B all completed experiments are listed. The experiments were conducted to see what combination of hyperparameters can produce the best result within the time constraint. The experiments either increase the number of estimators, the depth of the trees or the percentage of the training dataset (if this was present, as the number of estimators is not a hyperparameter for some algorithms, such as the regression tree).

4.5. Gradient boosting
While gradient boosting is also based on the generation of a decision tree, in this case the premise is that not all decisions made in the tree are equally good and generate an equal accuracy. Therefore, in gradient boosting, the algorithm starts with producing the first tree. For each prediction, it looks at the error margin produced by that prediction. If the predictions have a low error, the data in that subset are easy to fit/ classify and do not need further attention. However, subsets with a higher error margin are more difficult to fit/classify and need further refining to find a better fit/classification. This distinction is done by the algorithm by assigning a low weight to predictions with a low error and a higher weight to those with high error. Those with the highest weight are then reevaluated in a subsequent tree, to produce a better fit. This second tree is added to the first tree, thereby improving the first tree. This is done in as many iterations as has been set in the algorithm. The hyperparameters in this algorithm are the same as for the random forest algorithms. First, there is the number of estimators—in this case the iterations producing new trees—added to the last produced tree. Second there is the depth of the tree, which is the number of branches in the tree. Finally, there is the percentage of the training dataset used. These three hyperparameters must be tested and optimized to find a solution that has the highest accuracy and a calculation time of approximately 12 h.

6. Discussion
The results of our study suggest that, compared to our ground truth dataset, 1) the TABULA model for energy labelling has limited accuracy, and 2) machine learning algorithms are significantly more accurate in energy labelling housing, even when using open data only. As a public policy instrument, there are constraints on data and method. Public policy instruments require transparency and hence only transparent algorithms can be applied. Other machine learning methods such as deep learning, or very deep and wide decision tree-based models, can potentially appear as a ‘black box’, in which case they lack the necessary transparency. Similarly, the data on which models are based must be open data available for the entire country. Zirak et al. [16] have shown that for some countries this could create a challenge, but initiatives as the INSPIRE initiative [21] aim to address this. Finally, calculation time was restrained to 12 h on a standard business pc, to represent viable situations for implementation. We, nevertheless, achieved an accuracy of 71%, significantly higher than the 26% of the TABULA model. The 26% accuracy of the TABULA model is low compared to the best performing machine learning algorithm, but not inconceivable, as the machine learning algorithm uses more data and more advanced modelling techniques. One possible explanation for this could be that the building typologies were defined in a report created in December 2010 [39] and the building inventory used to generate the typologies has since changed. Especially since the signing of the Dutch Energy Agreement by the Dutch government and its partners in 2013 [40], the energy performance of the stock of non-profit rental dwellings has shown improvement, even though progress by this group was lower than required to reach set goals prior to 2014 (Filippidou, 2017). Therefore, the typologies in TABULA [11] may nowadays overestimate energy consumption and underestimate level of insulation of buildings. Also, the accuracy of the models depends on the quality of the input and the test data. The BAG and CBS sources for the input data have undergone rigorous checks by the Dutch government agencies that supply the open data. Jenkins et al. [41], have indicated that the quality of the EPCs gathered by auditors can very significantly within a country. Therefore, the RVO database of EPC’s used for the test data was researched for accuracy by the Dutch Court of Auditors [31] and found to have an accuracy of 86%. However, the RVO database of EPC’s is only a subset of all dwellings in the Netherlands. This subset has been evaluated for representativeness for all parameters that were available. The rules for when an EPC must be obtained, have allowed people to opt out of obtaining a label. After 2008, it was mandatory to obtain a label only when a building was sold, or when a new lease was signed for a rental building—and initially this rule carried no penalty if not observed. After receiving a tentative energy label (based on the TABULA building typologies), homeowners were encouraged to invest in an on-site survey to correct their energy label if they undertook additional energy saving measures, such as solar panels or insulation. This procedure makes it conceivable that some homeowners who had a better label than modelled changed their tentative label with a formal EPC, while others did not. The input data used in this study were selected to provide likely proxies for the building characteristics used to determine the energy performance—for instance, window surface area and insulation values. However, as Majcen et al. [42] have shown, building characteristics do not always accurately predict associated energy consumption. As the label is used to inform the prospective homeowner or tenant of the energy requirement of a building, as well as its prospective energy savings, it is important to account for elements that could explain this difference. Therefore, the social parameters of a dwelling—such as household income, number of occupants, or ownership (attempted at a higher scale in this study)—could play a role. Note that such parameters are generally less available as open data. Furthermore, the Dutch position on building information is very high. There is a bounty of accurate open data available to researchers and policymakers to do analyses. Future research could look at an open data strategy that considers open data available in a wider range of countries. For instance, researchers could replicate this approach using open data made accessible through the INSPIRE initiative in all of Europe.
7. Conclusion
In conclusion, the method of open data coupled with machine learning, as proposed in this study, is a suitable solution for modelling energy labels for the entire building stock. This seems a very fruitful alternative, as this study shows that current accuracy of energy labels modelled with the TABULA building typologies [11] is only 26% for the Netherlands. Using the method designed in this study allows policymakers to benefit from an accessible and transparent modelling strategy
to increase insight into the energy performance of the building stock. Open data availability might differ between different municipalities or countries. Some countries might have more specific data available, while in other countries not all data used in this study would be available as open data. To create this method, the authors gathered a large database of open data for all residential buildings that can serve as proxy for the thermodynamic balance of the properties. This database contains the training data that is used to create the AI models for predicting energy labels and is comprised of data gathered for each building in the Netherlands under the highest quality control by the Dutch government. The buildings used for training data were restricted to those that have an audited energy label, which are over 2.5 million residential buildings spread through the entire country and containing a representative sample of the Dutch building stock (no representativity of climate zone is required, as the Netherlands has only one climate zone). In this study 7 different machine learning algorithms were tested with a variety of hyperparameters for each of the algorithms. The bestperforming algorithm was the random forest algorithm, with an accuracy of 71% (a significant increase from the 26% of the TABULA model). Furthermore, this study evaluated whether the algorithm indicated relevant but not accurate energy performance information by evaluating what the accuracy was when allowing to deviate one label (e.g. it is a “B” label, but the model gave either an “A” or a “C” label). When evaluating these numbers, the TABULA model provided an accuracy of 67%, while the random forest machine learning algorithm provided an accuracy of 93%. These results showed the relevance of this methodology for EPC modelling using open data and machine learning.
