ABSTRACT
The built environment significantly contributes to global energy use and CO2 emissions, with
large-scale energy efficiency evaluations often hindered by cost and data inconsistencies. This
study employs zero-shot learning with Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to classify building
energy efficiency from images. Our methodology integrates advanced image processing with
natural language understanding, enabling buildings to be classified into UK Energy Performance
Certificate (EPC) grades based solely on visual inputs. By leveraging pre-trained knowledge in
VLMs, the framework identifies energy efficiency levels using descriptive attributes without
requiring prior training on labeled examples. Performance evaluation against traditional
supervised learning models demonstrates VLMs can effectively categorize buildings into energy
efficiency classes. Results highlight VLMs' potential as a scalable tool for building energy
assessment to inform renovation planning and sustainable urban development.
INTRODUCTION
The buildings and construction sectors play a crucial role in addressing climate change. Together,
they account for approximately 30% of global final energy consumption and 27% of total CO2


Proceedings Paper Formatting Instructions – 2 – Rev. 10/2015
emissions from the energy sector (IEA 2023). To support the United Nations' Sustainable
Development Goals, particularly in advancing energy efficiency in buildings and accelerating the
decarbonization process (Arora and Mishra 2019), many governments worldwide have
implemented similar regulations. One common measure is requiring an energy performance
certificate (EPC) for properties when they are sold or rented. In the European Union (EU), Under
the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (2010/31/EU), all EU member states are required
to establish independent control systems for EPCs (EC 2010). In the United Kingdom, the Ministry
of Housing, Communities, and Local Government established the Energy Performance of
Buildings (England and Wales) Regulations 2012 to ensure compliance with energy efficiency
standards (Regulations 2012).
Evaluating the overall energy performance of buildings is a complex process, requiring
consideration of multiple factors, such as the building type, age, number of habitable rooms, type
and amount of glazing, construction materials, and wall insulation, among others (DCLG 2017).
Collecting such detailed data is challenging, as it often involves substantial costs and effort,
especially on a large scale. Most models for energy efficiency estimation require specific
information about individual homes and communities, which is either unavailable or too expensive
to obtain at large scale (Streltsov et al. 2020). This demands faster and more accessible methods
to determine building energy efficiency levels. While energy-related predictions from historical or
tabular data have proven effective, applying these methods on a large scale remains challenging
due to the extensive workload required to prepare the datasets. Estimating building energy
efficiency grades using imagery has emerged as a solution, especially with advancements in
imagery collection technologies (e.g., street view and satellite images) and the application of deep
learning for object recognition in recent years (Mayer et al. 2023). Since the building envelope 
comprising facade walls, windows, and roof – significantly impacts energy efficiency, enhancing
its thermal insulation properties is recognized as a key strategy for improving overall building
energy performance (Nicolae and George-Vlad 2015). This relationship enables reasonable
inference of building envelope properties from visual data to estimate energy efficiency levels.
Many previous studies have used supervised learning approaches, curated custom datasets
as input and trained classifiers, particularly deep learning models, to predict the energy efficiency
levels of buildings. A commonly used approach is transfer learning (Weiss et al. 2016), which
involves leveraging pre-trained models – initially trained on large, general-purpose datasets – and
re-training them on custom datasets tailored to specific tasks (Pan et al. 2022). However, a
significant challenge in this process is obtaining enough annotated image data to train models
effectively for individual objectives. Pre-trained Large Language Models (LLMs), such as
ChatGPT (OpenAI 2022), have demonstrated exceptional performance in natural language
processing (NLP) tasks. With the advancement of large Visual Language Models (VLMs), such
as GPT-4o (OpenAI 2024), these models now offer a different solution for addressing image
interpretation tasks. By integrating visual and linguistic capabilities, pre-trained VLMs enable
information extraction and analysis of image data.

In this study, we utilize VLMs to predict building energy efficiency grades based on facade
images. This approach utilizes the capabilities of large pre-trained VLMs to interpret visual data
and offers a novel way of addressing the challenges associated with the limited availability of
annotated datasets for energy efficiency prediction tasks.
BACKGROUND
Deep learning-based approaches have been extensively developed to estimate energy
related characteristics of buildings. Tabular data is one of the commonly utilized data sources
in this domain. For example, energy consumption has been predicted using the random forest
algorithm on datasets of hourly building energy consumption (Pham et al. 2020). Image data serves
as another key source of information for energy prediction. Overhead images, such as satellite and
aerial images, are often employed in deep learning frameworks to segment and classify buildings;
random forest regression models can then be applied to features extracted by neural networks to
estimate energy consumption (Streltsov et al. 2020). A similar approach integrates contextual
information from urban texture with satellite imagery to enhance energy consumption predictions
using features derived from satellite images (Dougherty et al. 2021).
Additionally, facade images combined with other factors, such as structural and
morphological attributes, are used to improve the predictive performance of deep learning models
(Sun et al. 2022). Moreover, diverse data sources, including aerial and street view images, have
been leveraged to train classifiers for predicting electricity consumption of buildings (Rosenfelder
et al. 2021) or for categorizing buildings into energy efficiency classes (Mayer et al. 2023).
Multiple data sources generally yield better results than single data sources, as they allow the
accumulation of information across modalities. Pre-trained Visual Language Models (VLMs)
that process both images and text inputs have demonstrated their effectiveness across various
fields. Notable models like CLIP (Shen et al. 2021), BLIP (Li et al. 2022), and GLIP (Li et al.
2022) have excelled in visual understanding through zero-shot learning, matching or even
exceeding the performance of traditional computer vision models. The Architecture, Engineering,
and Construction (AEC) domain offers numerous applications for such approaches, which is why
research in this area is rapidly evolving. For example, GPT-4 Vision has been successfully
employed to predict building age classifications from facade images, demonstrating its ability to
make accurate predictions without the need for specialized training (Zeng et al. 2024).
In conclusion, the significant advantages and flexibility of using large pre-trained VLMs
without requiring training data present vast potential for predicting building energy efficiency.
This paper proposes a framework that utilizes VLMs to predict energy efficiency grades from
street-view images of buildings. The research contributions of this paper are as follows: a) We
propose a framework that utilizes large VLMs to predict the energy efficiency level of building
facades from street view images, focusing specifically on facade-level analysis rather than whole
building assessment. b) To evaluate the efficacy of the proposed approach, we conduct extensive

experiments and compare the performance of our framework against a traditional supervised
learning setup.
METHODOLOGY
This study estimates building envelope energy efficiency in Cambridge, UK, using a
custom dataset. Images are obtained using the Google Street View Static API (Google 2024);
energy efficiency ratings are sourced from the Energy Performance of Buildings Register
(MHCLG 2020), which includes EPCs for properties in England and Wales from October 1, 2008,
onward. As previously mentioned, numerous factors influence a building's energy efficiency.
However, street view imagery provides information limited to the building envelope.
Consequently, we focus on extracting energy efficiency ratings specifically for windows
(WINDOWS_ENERGY_EFF) and walls (WALLS_ENERGY_EFF) from the EPC data to label
the images appropriately. These envelope-specific ratings provide a targeted basis for analyzing
the relationship between visible facade features and energy performance. The process of creating
the dataset is illustrated in Figure 1. This process begins with inputting building addresses from
the Energy Performance of Buildings Register database. Next, we use the Google Street View API
to verify whether imagery exists for the specified locations. Following this, we compare the
timestamp of the imagery captured by Google with the assessment date of the buildings. Since
both datasets span long periods, only images captured within one year of the building assessment
date are retained. This procedure results in a total of 9,720 images.
Subsequently, we manually refine this dataset by removing unsuitable images.
Specifically, we exclude images that 1) show buildings located far from the point of view, 2) have
high levels of occlusion caused by traffic or vegetation, or 3) contain multiple buildings in the field
of view. This manual curation ensures the quality and relevance of the dataset for further analysis
and results in a dataset with 5,615 images, split into 4,371 images for training and 1,244 images
for testing (approximately an 80/20 split). The corresponding labels for the energy efficiency
ratings of walls are categorized as "very good", "good", "average", "poor", and "very poor". It is
important to note that the training set is used to compute a baseline, providing a reference against
which the predictions of the VLM can be evaluated.
Figure 1. Process of preparing dataset.
Based on the dataset we have prepared, we propose an approach to identify the energy
efficiency ratings using the large pre-trained VLM GPT-4o model. This classifier is zero-shot (Liu
and Tuytelaars 2020), and which does not require a training process.

There are two types of inputs to the method: a prompt and an image. We use xx to represent
the image and yy to represent the prompt. The VLM comprises two main modules: a visual module,
denoted as VV(xx), which processes the image input, and a semantic module, represented as SS(yy),
which processes the text of the prompt. These two modules each generate high-dimensional
embeddings, which are subsequently combined and processed by further network structures to
generate the response in text form. The prompt specifies the requirements for the response,
including predictions of the energy efficiency level and an explanation of the reasoning behind the
decision. This process can be mathematically represented as:
(ll, RR) = PP(VV(xx), SS(yy)),
where ll is the predicted label (e.g., the energy efficiency level of walls), RR is the reasoning
text explaining the decision, and PP represents the network modules that process the combined
visual and semantic embeddings to produce the final output. Two types of input are required here.
The input images have been encoded in Base64 format for further processing with GPT-4o models.
The input prompts are texts of two types: 1) the system prompt, a predefined prompt that controls
the structure and content of the output and 2) the user prompt, a prompt that specifies a question
and task for the model. Both prompts play critical roles in shaping the final response generated by
the system. The system prompt and user prompt used in this study are depicted in Figure 2 and
Figure 3, respectively.

EXPERIMENT AND RESULTS
In this study, we evaluate the predictive performance of the proposed framework by
utilizing large pre-trained VLMs on a custom test set. As this approach is zero-shot, no additional
training on images is performed. To assess its effectiveness, we compare its results with those of
well-established neural network architectures commonly used for image classification tasks,
including three versions of ResNet (He et al. 2015) and three versions of the Vision Transformer
(ViT) (Dosovitskiy et al. 2020), both with and without pre-trained weights. Benchmark training
was conducted on the introduced custom dataset for 100 epochs on an NVIDIA A100 GPU with
80 GB of memory, while inference using GPT-4o was performed on a Linux subsystem running

on a Windows desktop. The cost of zero-shot inference using GPT-4o API for approximately 1,200
images was around USD 3.60.

When the problem is reformulated into a more generalized classification setup – combining
"Very Good" and "Good" into a single "Good" category, and "Very Poor" and "Poor" into a single

that the prediction accuracy of the GPT-4o model improves significantly to 65.6%. This
improvement can also be observed from the corresponding confusion matrix, as illustrated in

capability to classify the energy efficiency levels of walls based on image data. Although its
performance does not match that of supervised deep learning models, it provides an efficient and
cost-effective method for estimating energy efficiency without requiring any additional training.

CONCLUSION
This paper introduces a novel framework that leverages large pre-trained models to predict
the energy efficiency levels of building facades from street view images. This zero-shot approach
can not only predict energy efficiency levels but also provide reasons for its decisions without
requiring any specific training data. Given its independence from custom datasets, the framework
can be readily applied to large-scale datasets, such as images captured in cities worldwide. This
capability makes it a valuable tool for stakeholders by offering insights into the energy efficiency
of entire buildings, ultimately aiding in the reduction of energy consumption. Future work may
include enriching the framework with additional data for an end-to-end estimation of whole
buildings. Furthermore, fine-tuning large language models could be explored to enhance prediction
accuracy.
