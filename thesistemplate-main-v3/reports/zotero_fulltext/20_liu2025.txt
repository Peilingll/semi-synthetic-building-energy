ABSTRACT
Building energy efficiency is critical for carbon neutrality, yet rapid, large-scale performance assessment remains challenging. This study introduces a three-stage multimodal framework combining Google Street View (GSV) imagery and GIS data to predict building-level Energy Performance Certificates (EPC), wall ratings, and Energy Use Intensity (EUI). First, facade extraction optimizes GSV camera viewpoints using GIS coordinates, segmenting key architectural features. Second, a deep learning model classifies wall performance (star ratings: very poor–very good) and EPC grades (A–G), incorporating shooting distance/angle analysis to enhance feature discrimination. Third, a pretrained energy model integrates building attributes with deep learning outputs, validated through transfer learning on 1 % local samples (e.g., 120 buildings) across UK cities. The framework achieves 84 % EPC accuracy and 76 % wall rating precision, with Grad-CAM interpretability identifying critical facade features—surface degradation, solar panels, and material patterns. Terraced houses (<7.95 m height) yield highest accuracy when facades occupy > 25 % of images, reducing regional EUI errors (CVRMSE) by 48–52 % versus conventional methods. Transfer learning adapts models to median-scale Middle Super Output Area (MSOA) blocks, outperforming in pre-1980 structures but facing limitations in urban high-rises due to occlusions. This scalable approach enables cost-effective retrofit prioritization and policy-driven decarbonization using widely accessible visual data.
1. Introduction
The world continues to face significant energy consumption challenges, and countries around the globe have introduced various policies to achieve the carbon neutrality goal by 2050 [1]. Energy efficiency and carbon reduction in buildings are crucial steps toward achieving carbon neutrality[2]. According to data from 2022, the operational energy consumption of buildings accounts for 30 % of global final energy consumption and 26 % of global energy-related emissions [1,3,4]. The residential sector accounts for a significant portion of carbon emissions, taking UK as an example, which makes up 19.9 % of the UK’s total emissions in 2021 [5]. Retrofitting existing residential buildings for energy efficiency is one of the key methods to reduce operational energy consumption [6,7]. However, before conducting retrofitting, a comprehensive assessment of a building’s energy performance is required. How to conduct a thorough and rapid assessment of the energy consumption of existing residential buildings has become a challenge. Traditional building energy analysis methods face significant
limitations: while physics-based simulations can predict system performance under untested conditions, they require extensive input parameters that are often difficult to obtain due to privacy concerns and come with high costs and time consumption [8,9]. In contrast, data-driven machine learning approaches can make predictions based on simplified features but demand stringent requirements for training data quality [10]. To address these challenges, this study incorporates street view imagery to enhance model performance and leverages explainable AI (XAI) techniques to enable “machine teaching” by decoding model decision pathways to reveal micro-scale feature correlations between building facade textures and energy performance indicators that are imperceptible to human vision, thereby filling a critical research gap. This integrated approach combining multi-source data with explainable analytics establishes a new paradigm for building energy consumption prediction. The overarching goal of this research aims to use Google Street View images and GIS data to non-invasively estimate the building Energy Performance rating and Energy Use Intensity (EUI) of buildings, thereby

2. Background
2.1. Development of building energy modeling
With the acceleration of urbanization, the importance of city-scale energy simulation and regional energy consumption assessment has become increasingly prominent. Building energy calculation methods based on white-box models have gradually evolved from early simulations of individual buildings to comprehensive simulations at the community and even city scale. Table A1 in Appendix summarizes recent simulation-based energy calculation models or platforms developed in recent years[11], including EnergyPlus [12,13], CitySim [14], SimStadt [15], Umi [16], UBEM.io [17], OPENIDEAS [18] , City Energy Analyst (CEA)[19], AutoBPS-Prototype [20], and others. Driven by technological innovation, these models are rapidly advancing toward greater automation and integration. For example, CitySim [14] and Umi [16] integrate various tools and simulation engines to provide more accurate and efficient simulations. Subsequently, more user-friendly models and software platforms were developed, such as UBEM.io [17] and AutoBPSPrototype [20], which through web-based frameworks and graphical user interfaces, enabled the rapid, automated generation of building energy models, supporting user customization and expanding to urbanscale energy consumption and carbon emission scenario studies. Additionally, UAV-BIM-BEM [21] as an enhanced AutoBPS-BIM tool converts drone point cloud models into building energy models for energy consumption calculations. At the same time, more models and software platforms have opened up data standards and adopted multi-tool collaboration methods, such as CityBES [22–25] and OPENIDEAS [26], enhancing data exchange and the interoperability of models. However, despite the increasing level of automation, white-box models still face inherent limitations due to their reliance on physicsbased computations. They depend on complex system details, including mathematics, building physics dynamics, and the specifics of all components, and access is often restricted due to privacy concerns
[8]. Moreover, when a large number of solutions need to be defined, the monetary and time cost can be very high [27]. In comparison, some data-driven machine learning models offer advantages such as lower computational resource requirements and faster processing times. Additionally, the development of building protypes has become a mainstream approach for estimating urban-scale energy demand. By grouping buildings with similar characteristics into a limited number of typical archetypes[28], this method simplifies complex urban building stocks and avoids repetitive modeling of individual structures, greatly improving computational efficiency.
2.2. Computer vision and Machine teaching in urban and architectural scenario
As a key branch of artificial intelligence, computer vision enables machines to understand and analyze visual data such as images and videos, providing powerful technical support for non-intrusive and efficient large-scale studies [29]. This technology has significantly lowered the professional entry barrier in fields like industrial inspection and medical diagnostics, while demonstrating great potential in urban and architectural applications. Traditionally labor-intensive and timeconsuming tasks—such as building heat loss detection [30,31], material recognition [32–34], building height estimation [35], and road condition assessment [36,37]—can now be automated and rapidly analyzed using deep learning based on visual data. Moreover, since model inputs are no longer limited to structured data, open-source and web-based resources like Google Street View have been effectively integrated, enhancing model prediction capabilities and applicability through multimodal fusion. Simultaneously, with the breakthrough advancements in Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI)[38], the field of machine learning is witnessing the emergence of a transformative research direction known as Machine Teaching. Numerous empirical studies have shown that in specialized fields like crack detection[39], medical image analysis[40], and industrial quality control[41], machine learning models not only outperform human experts in accuracy but also identify subtle features and latent patterns that traditional visual methods fail to detect. This “machine teaching” mechanism, which reverse-engineers model decision paths, offers a new perspective on visual feature cognition. It is reshaping how expert knowledge is generated, facilitating deeper and broader understanding of complex phenomena. In architecture, this technology is used for detecting equipment faults[42], structural damage[43], and similar issues. Despite widespread application of image-based deep learning combined with explainability algorithms across various fields, research specifically focused on detecting building envelope features and determining energy performance ratings remains underdeveloped. This technical gap restricts the application of AI in building energy evaluations and optimization efforts, requiring more systematic and interdisciplinary approaches to be addressed.
2.3. Improving interpretability and generalization through image processing and transfer learning
In previous studies, deep learning techniques have been used to predict building energy efficiency ratings or electricity consumption in cities such as Glasgow, UK [44], Gainesville and San Diego, USA [45]. However, these studies were mostly limited to specific cities. When using smaller image datasets, deep learning models are prone to overfitting[29], and their generalization ability may decrease when applied to different regions. For example, when predicting building age, the model’s generalization performance often declines as the geographic scope expands [46,47]. Additionally, factors such as image resolution, differences in camera angles, feature shift, and background interference can also affect the accuracy and applicability of the model. 2.4. Research objective
In the field of city-scale building energy assessment, traditional physics-based simulation methods offer strong interpretability but face inherent limitations that hinder their large-scale applicability. These include complex modeling processes, high computational costs, and dependence on extensive input data—much of which is difficult to obtain and may involve privacy-sensitive information. In contrast, machine learning approaches based on limited feature sets address some of these issues but still struggle with prediction accuracy.
Against this backdrop, computer vision offers a promising avenue for expanding input data modalities and improving prediction performance. Furthermore, interpretability-driven “machine teaching” mechanisms can uncover subtle, discriminative visual features in building facades that are often imperceptible to humans—thus enabling a new paradigm of knowledge discovery. This study proposes an innovative large-scale energy use prediction framework based on facade imagery. By leveraging publicly available image sources such as Google Street View, we collect building facade data and apply a series of image enhancement techniques (including image background removal) to improve data quality. A transfer learning strategy is employed to enhance the model’s adaptability across regions, effectively addressing key challenges such as overfitting and regional heterogeneity that often undermine generalizability. The core objectives of this research are as follows: 1) To develop a building energy consumption prediction framework that incorporates easily accessible street-view imagery as additional features, aiming to improve the performance of building energy models; 2) To apply Grad-CAM interpretability analysis in order to identify discriminative micro-level visual features within building facades and facilitate knowledge discovery through machine teaching; 3) To enhance model generalizability and cross-regional adaptability through a synergistic integration of image processing and transfer learning; 4) To systematically explore optimal image-capturing parameters and identify suitable building types, thereby providing practical guidance for the engineering application of rapid building energy modeling

3. Data and methodology
The research is divided into three main steps, as shown in Fig. 1: First, facade images are extracted using GIS data and optimal street-view camera points, with key buildings segmented. Next, a deep learning classification model is trained using wall star ratings (from very poor to very good) and EPC energy efficiency grades (A-G) as labels, while analyzing the impact of shooting distance and angles. Finally, a pretrained energy consumption model is developed by integrating building attribute data with deep learning results, validated through transfer learning on median-scale MSOA blocks in cities including Newcastle.
3.1. Data Introduction
This study integrates multiple data sources, including the EPC data from England [53], the UKBuildings dataset from the EDINA Geomni Digimap Service[54], and Google Street View images used for estimating building energy efficiency.
3.1.1. Pretrained model building sample sources
In this study, we randomly selected 200 buildings from each EPC rating (A-G) in 117 cities across England. The residential types include commonly found housing types in the UK, such as terraced houses, detached houses, semi-detached houses, and various types of apartment buildings, to train a model with a certain level of accuracy for use across England.
3.1.2. EPC data
Energy Performance Certificates (EPC) are tools used to assess and communicate the energy efficiency of buildings[55,56]. These certificates provide valuable information on a building’s energy consumption and environmental impact, helping to raise awareness and promote energy efficiency, particularly in the residential sector [57,58].In the UK, approximately 50 % of buildings in the UK have an EPC, and obtaining an EPC assessment is required during the sale, rental, or new construction process. The EPC has influenced decisions regarding building renovations [59]. The publicly available EPC information includes various details on a building’s energy efficiency, such as its energy performance, potential energy efficiency, wall ratings, and ratings for household appliances like heating, hot water, air conditioning, and lighting systems. EPC assessors record the specific details of buildings through surveys and on-site visits, documenting aspects such as building size, layout, construction type, insulation, and systems for heating, ventilation, and lighting. Using this detailed information, they estimate the building’s monthly energy usage and CO2 emissions based on government-approved calculation methods. Finally, buildings are rated according to their energy efficiency level. Additionally, the EPC data provides performance ratings for building walls, windows, and roofs, as well as information on whether the property is equipped with a photovoltaic system. It also includes the total estimated energy consumption
of the property over a 12-month period (measured in kWh/m2), presented as the current primary energy use per square meter of floor area, offering a key reference for building energy efficiency assessment. The study randomly selected 200 samples for each EPC rating (A, B, C, D, E, F, or G) in each city from EPC records spanning the past 10 years (from 2014 to the present). To ensure uniformity across different EPC ratings and cities, 200 samples were chosen per rating in each city, resulting in a total of nearly 200,000 samples for the training set. By converting addresses into coordinates via the UPRN (Unique Property Reference Number) and its location, the corresponding geographic location for each EPC record was obtained.
3.1.3. Ukbuildings GIS data
The UKBuildings dataset provides 2D building footprint data for residential, non-residential, and mixed-use properties across various regions in UK[60]. This dataset also includes information such as building height and construction year for each building. This dataset is an important geographical reference resource that can be used to match street view images that describe the appearance of the building. The dataset offers detailed descriptions of the building layout and structure across cities and towns in the UK, including residential areas, commercial areas, and mixed-use zones. These data are crucial for identifying key information such as building types, sizes, and locations. By matching EPC records with the UK Buildings dataset, we were able to determine the building’s footprint, use, and the corresponding street view facade. EPC records were also used to map the buildings onto the 2D plane in the UKBuildings dataset. Each building footprint may correspond to multiple EPC records. In this case, we select the most frequent (or the one with the largest area) EPC rating and wall rating as the representative data for that building footprint. As a result, each building footprint is considered the basic unit for energy consumption calculation.
3.1.4. Google street view images
Street view images, as digital representations of the built environment, are an important resource for understanding and analyzing buildings and cities[44]. With the expanding coverage and the increase in service providers, street view image services have now covered most countries around the world [61]. Street view images provide a more realistic and immersive perspective, allowing people to gain a deeper understanding of buildings and urban environments. Among all street view image services, Google Street View (GSV) is one of the most popular and widely used resources[62].
3.2. Data processing
3.2.1. Street view image processing
The method for selecting building facades and corresponding street view images for the training set is as follows. The main selection principle is to choose street view images that are closer to the building and more directly facing the facade, ensuring clearer images and minimizing perspective distortion of the building facade. The specific steps are as follows, as illustrated in Fig. 2: First, the geometric center of the building footprint for each residential building in the dataset is calculated. Using this center as the center of a circle, all panoramic image shooting points within a 20-meter radius are selected. Then, it is checked whether the line connecting these points to the geometric center is blocked by other buildings. Shooting points that are obstructed are eliminated. The radius is set to 20 m based on our experiments, which indicate that street view images captured from a distance greater than 20 m fail to provide sufficient building facade details necessary for deep learning models to make scientifically sound decisions. By restricting the selection to within this range, we ensure that the chosen images contain clearer architectural features and higher-quality details that enhance the model’s learning capability. Next, for the remaining shooting points, the intersection with the building facade is determined, and the field of view (FOV) angle formed between the facade and the street view shooting point is calculated. Then we calculated, for each unobstructed street view image within a 20-meter radius of the geometric center of the residence, the product of the squared sine of the angle θ between the image’s angle bisector direction and the building facade, and the distance d from the camera to the facade. The image with the highest value of this metric was selected for further processing. Given the input size constraints of the subsequent deep learning model, we prioritize images that are more directly facing the building facade, followed by those captured at closer distances. Based on this consideration, we used the product of the squared sine of θ (emphasizing frontal view alignment) and the linear distance d (ensuring sufficient clarity of the building in the image) as a weighting

3.2.2. The selection and comparison of deep learning framework
We improved the existing pre-trained deep convolutional neural network models, with the goal of better classifying building energy efficiency based on the energy types in EPC data. The classification targets the energy efficiency levels (A-G), using facade images as input. As shown in Fig. 5, the network consists of four key stages: input layer, feature extraction layer, feature fusion layer, and output layer. In the final layer, we reduce the output from the existing 1024-dimensional features to a number of levels corresponding to the energy efficiency categories. To compare existing deep learning architectures and select the best-performing model, we experimented with several commonly used deep learning frameworks, including Densenet, Resnet, ResNext, Vit, and Vgg. During training, the images were split into 80 % for the training set and 20 % for the test set, with each model being trained for 120 epochs. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) is a method used to interpret the prediction results of image deep learning models[64]. It improves upon traditional Class Activation Mapping (CAM) without altering the architecture of the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). Instead, it uses the gradient information of the target class flowing to the final convolutional layer of the model and generates a heatmap through weighted averaging[65]. This heatmap highlights the regions of the image that are most important for the prediction result. To analyze the model’s predictive mechanisms, this study employs the PyTorch-GradCAM toolkit in Python for interpretability
analysis. The algorithm generates pixel-level weight distributions through gradient-based weighted calculations of target categories. To effectively differentiate discriminative regions across various classes, we perform multiplicative operations between each category’s pixel weights and their corresponding predicted probabilities (as detailed in Fig. S1 in Supplementary Materials), thereby visually demonstrating the differential contributions of distinct image regions to predictions across different categories.
3.2.4. Prediction and validation of EUI
This study integrates the softmax outputs of three image-based deep learning models with the EUI baseline derived from building prototypes—defined by key features such as building age, dwelling type, footprint area, and building form—to train a CatBoost model for predicting residential Energy Use Intensity (EUI), as showed in Fig. 5. We also validated the model’s generalization ability in different regions, with the aim of extending its application from a small regional sample to the entire city in future real-world applications. The study selected three large cities in England—Newcastle, Birmingham, and Bristol—as test areas. For each city, building data from an MSOA (Middle Super Output Area) with a building count close to the median was selected as the sample data for training the pretrained CatBoost model. This study employs a transfer learning strategy that fine-tunes a pretrained CatBoost model with limited local samples to significantly enhance the model’s generalization capability in target regions. Unlike the weight freezing approach commonly used in deep learning, our method adopts model parameter initialization (init_model) as the core transfer mechanism. This approach utilizes the pre-trained model’s parameters as initial values for the new model, preserving existing knowledge while further optimizing model performance through local data. 4. Result and analysis
4.1. Performance of image classification models
Fig. 6 shows the prediction performance of the trained image classification models. The first column displays the prediction results for walls, the second column shows the prediction results for EPC ratings. In terms of feature processing, we have appropriately simplified the original rating classification based on the following two main considerations: First, this decision was primarily driven by the practical limitation of imbalanced data distribution. Highly skewed class distributions make it difficult for models to achieve statistically robust performance on minority classes and often lead to overfitting on majority classes—an observation supported by prior studies on energy efficiency ratings[44,66]. Second, the simplified three-level classification system better aligns with real-world policy formulation and decision-making needs. The confusion matrices for each individual category, along with the methodology for category consolidation are attached in Fig. S2 in Supplementary Materials. Taking EPC ratings as an example, our classification strategy draws on the UK government’s practice in energy poverty surveys[67], where categories A, B, and C are often grouped together. However, considering the UK government’s policy goal that “all rental properties must achieve a minimum EPC rating of C by 2030”[68,69], we grouped all buildings below grade C into a single category (representing those in urgent need of energy retrofitting), treated grade C as a standalone category (as a key policy target), and merged grades A and B into one category. A similar simplification was applied to wall ratings: one-star ratings were classified as “Poor,” twostar to four-star as “Average,” and five-star as “Good.” This classification approach ensures the model remains computationally tractable while aligning with policy-relevant energy performance thresholds. It can be observed that the results from Birmingham are relatively better, with wall prediction accuracy reaching 75 %, and the prediction accuracy for EPC rating categories also at 75 %. In wall performance prediction, it is found that the worse the wall performance, the higher
the prediction accuracy. For example, in Bristol, the prediction accuracy for walls classified as “poor” reaches 95 %, and in Newcastle, it reaches 91 %. In EPC rating prediction, similarly, buildings with poorer performance are predicted more accurately. In all three cities, the prediction accuracy for buildings classified as “D and below” in Bristol reaches 92 %. Overall, the model performs more accurately in predicting lower wall grades and EPC bands. The explainability analysis also reveals that the model tends to focus more on specific features of poorly performing residences. While the results in some cities, like Newcastle, are not as ideal, these findings still demonstrate that the model is capable of performing a “diagnostic” task—identifying residential buildings that urgently require energy-efficient retrofitting.
4.2. Exploration of boundary conditions for image classification prediction accuracy
To explore the boundary conditions for the applicability of the image classification model, we analyzed the impact of various building factors, shooting conditions, and geographic distribution on the prediction accuracy of image classification. By combining XGBoost with decision tree methods for parameter clustering, we identified groups with higher prediction accuracy and those with poorer prediction performance. Fig. 7 shows the main parameters used, where φ represents the horizontal field of view, θ represents the angle between the line connecting the shooting point and the center of the facade and the facade itself, d represents the distance between the shooting point and the building facade, S represents the building’s footprint area, and H represents the building’s average height. Fig. 8 and Fig. 9 show the prediction accuracy for wall performance performance and EPC rating for different clustering groups. The left images represent building factors, while the right images represent shooting condition factors. The vertical axis of the images represents the proportion of sample quantities in each clustering group, while the horizontal axis represents prediction accuracy. Therefore, the darker areas in each image represent the proportion of correct predictions within that category. Fig. 8 illustrates the prediction accuracy for wall performance across various building clusters. The accuracy tends to be higher for buildings constructed later and with fewer floors. Specifically, residential buildings built after 1980 and with three or fewer floors (under 7.95 m) exhibit the highest accuracy, reaching 82.4 %. In contrast, buildings with more than three floors show lower accuracy (66.9 %), likely due to the increased complexity of their structures. Older buildings, particularly those built before 1945, also have lower prediction accuracy, likely because of multiple repairs and renovations that alter the original wall performance, making predictions more challenging. Additionally, accuracy improves when the building occupies a larger pixel area in the image, when the shooting angle is more direct, and when the field of view exceeds 31.8◦. The highest accuracy (76.3 %) is achieved when the building facade covers more than 25 % of the image, whereas the lowest accuracy (56.9 %) occurs when the facade occupies less than 25 %. These findings highlight the importance of both building characteristics and imaging conditions in predicting wall performance. Fig. 9 presents the prediction accuracy for residential EPC ratings across different clustering groups. The left image shows that higher buildings generally exhibit higher prediction accuracy for EPC ratings. Specifically, residential buildings with an average height greater than 8.35 m and a footprint area smaller than 58.05 square meters achieve the highest EPC prediction accuracy of 75.2 %. In contrast, buildings with a height below 6.95 m and a footprint area greater than 128.15 square meters have the lowest accuracy, at 64.5 %. 4.4. Prediction results of EUI
Fig. 13 presents the results of combining deep learning model outputs with additional features, followed by transfer learning on 1 % of samples from each MSOA (Middle Layer Super Output Area) using the CatBoost model. This study adopts a sample ratio of 1 % for transfer learning to simulate real-world constraints in urban residential big data applications, where acquiring large-scale labeled samples is often impractical. To evaluate the model’s generalization ability under limited data conditions, we propose an inverse 100-fold cross-validation strategy. Specifically, the local dataset is evenly divided into 100 subsets. In each iteration, one subset (1 %) is used to fine-tune the pre-trained model, while the remaining 99 subsets (99 %) serve as an independent test set to assess performance. This validation approach is designed to rigorously reflect the challenges of sample scarcity in real-world applications. At the same time, by repeating the experiment 100 times, the method ensures the statistical robustness and reliability of the evaluation results. The first row presents the MedAPE (Median Absolute Percentage Error), indicating the typical level of prediction error. The second row shows the CVRMSE (Coefficient of Variation of Root Mean Squared Error), reflecting the variability of prediction errors. The third row illustrates the PSE (Percentage Sum Error), which captures the bias in estimating the total energy consumption at the regional level. Each column corresponds to the results for a different city, arranged from north to south: the first for Newcastle, the second for Birmingham, and the third for Bristol. The analysis highlights several key findings: error rates tend to be higher in city center areas compared to surrounding regions, with some outskirts of certain cities also exhibiting larger errors. Northern cities, such as Newcastle, generally show smaller errors compared to southern cities like Bristol, reflecting regional differences in prediction accuracy. For MedAPE, Newcastle’s MSOA blocks mostly have errors ranging between 17 % and 22 %, with a peak around 19.0 % (Fig. 13.a),
indicating that most energy use intensity (EUI) prediction errors for residential buildings fall within this range. In Birmingham, the MedAPE primarily ranges from 16 % to 23 %, with a similar peak at 18.0 % (Fig. 13.b), suggesting comparable prediction performance. However, in Bristol, the MedAPE range is broader, between 18 % and 25 %, with a peak around 21 % (Fig. 13.c), indicating that Bristol experiences relatively larger prediction errors. For CVRMSE, Newcastle’s MSOA blocks generally fall between 30 % and 42 %, with a peak at 33 % (Fig. 13.d). In Birmingham, the CVRMSE ranges from 29 % to 47 %, peaking again at around 33 % (Fig. 13.e). In contrast, Bristol’s CVRMSE ranges from 33 % to 55 %, with a peak at 38 % (Fig. 13.f), signaling higher prediction errors and lower model accuracy in Bristol. This suggests that northern cities, like Newcastle, tend to have narrower error distributions, while southern cities, like Bristol, show more variability and higher errors. For PSE (Percentage Sum Error), Newcastle’s errors range from 2 % to 7 %, with the peak at 4 % (Fig. 13.g). Birmingham’s errors range from 2.5 % to 8.5 %, with the peak at 4 % (Fig. 13.h). In Bristol, errors range from 2.5 % to 7 %, with the peak at 4 % (Fig. 13.i). These differences in PSE may be attributed to the higher proportion of buildings in northern England with better heating and insulation, suggesting that models predicting EUI based on building envelope performance and EPC (Energy Performance Certificate) levels are more accurate in the north. Overall, the results reflect that while northern cities tend to have more accurate energy consumption predictions, southern cities like Bristol face higher prediction errors, likely due to a combination of architectural, climatic, and socio-economic factors influencing building energy performance.

5.1. Contributions of model processing method improvements
This study introduces a method for predicting building energy consumption using publicly available data, specifically street view images. Unlike previous research that focuses on estimating energy efficiency based on unrefined image data, our approach employs rigorous image processing techniques to improve model performance and generalization. 5.3. Application scenarios
In the UK, only about half of the buildings have undergone professional EPC assessments, and evaluating the remaining buildings would involve a substantial amount of work. Additionally, residential energy consumption and energy bills are privacy-sensitive, making it a challenge to accurately estimate the annual energy consumption of individual homes using non-invasive methods in social surveys. Our model addresses these issues and explores the boundary conditions of its application. Research indicates that building characteristics and imaging conditions significantly affect the accuracy of wall and window performance predictions and EPC ratings. Low-rise buildings (under 7.95 m) built after 1980 have the highest wall prediction accuracy (82.4 %), while high-rise buildings have the lowest (66.9 %). Terraced houses and buildings with smaller base areas perform better in window predictions, while detached and semi-detached homes show lower accuracy. Imaging conditions also play a key role: prediction accuracy improves when the building occupies a larger portion of the image, is photographed from a closer distance, and with an aligned viewpoint. Wall prediction accuracy reaches 76.3 % when the building covers more than 25 % of the image, and window prediction is most accurate when window coverage is between 11 % and 68 %. Geographically, prediction errors are higher in city centers, especially in densely populated areas like Birmingham, due to building complexity and occlusions, whereas
suburban areas yield more consistent and accurate results. These insights suggest that future performance predictions should prioritize buildings constructed after 1980, with three or more stories, and located in urban centers. Photographic strategies should avoid facade occlusions and ensure the building covers over 25 % of the image, with window coverage between 11 % and 68 %, to improve accuracy. Considering that the high prediction errors for taller buildings are mainly due to the inability to capture complete facades, future work could employ drones or other aerial imaging methods to obtain full facade views, thereby mitigating the issue of excessive errors caused by incomplete visual information.
5.4. Limitations and future work
This study has made progress in technical feasibility and methodology, but some limitations remain. Although the model was trained with data from various climate zones, housing types, and architectural styles in England, its performance may be limited in regions outside of England. The model’s accuracy is also lower for high-rise buildings. Future work will integrate drone or high-resolution imagery to improve facade feature extraction and enhance performance for high-rise buildings and diverse urban environments. Due to limited access to sensor data, the model did not incorporate real-time monitoring or occupancy information, affecting its accuracy. However, relying solely on GIS data and street-view images still yields reasonable predictions. Future efforts will focus on collaborating with energy monitoring organizations to integrate sensor data for validation and complement traditional simulation methods. Although the deep learning model can identify common features between building facades and performance, these features do not apply to all cases. For example, some high-performance buildings may be misjudged as old buildings due to classical decorations, or when a facade image contains two buildings, the model may mistakenly use the prominent features of one to infer the performance of the other, as showed in Fig. 16. Therefore, model conclusions should be validated with expert judgment and additional data. Although this study follows privacy protocols (e.g., Google Street View’s blurring mechanism), large-scale building image analysis can still pose socio-economic bias risks. The model may reflect regional economic factors based on building density or facade differences, which could affect policy fairness. While privacy protection ensures compliance, it may impact prediction accuracy. Future work will involve collaborating with urban planning and public policy experts to create

6. Conclusion
This study proposes a novel framework that utilizes easily accessible Google Street View images and GIS data to estimate the Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) ratings and Energy Use Intensity (EUI) of buildings. This could reduce the cost of energy efficiency assessments and provide a non-invasive method for estimating residential annual energy consumption. The main conclusions of this study are as follows:
• Accuracy in predicting wall and EPC ratings, is influenced by building height, type, and imaging factors. Low-rise buildings (under 7.95 m) and terraced houses yield higher prediction accuracy, especially when the building occupies a larger portion of the image and the viewpoint is aligned. • To enhance energy consumption predictions, focus on minimizing occlusions, ensuring the building covers over 25 % of the image, and aligning the viewpoint. Lower accuracy was observed for buildings constructed after 1980, high-rise buildings, and detached houses in urban centers. • The model focuses on surface stains, peeling, and materials for wall performance and renewable energy indicators, like solar panels, for EPC ratings. Wall performance is influenced by surface stains, peeling, and specific materials, while EPC ratings improve with the identification of renewable energy features like solar panels. • By combining transfer learning and deep learning, the accuracy of energy consumption predictions across different regions was
significantly improved. When applied to Newcastle, Birmingham, and Bristol, particularly in areas with initially high error rates, the model reduced CVRMSE. By leveraging easily accessible Google Street View imagery and using only 1 % of local samples for transfer training, the model achieved a reduction of approximately 50 % in regional energy consumption estimation errors.
Overall, this study provides a cost-effective and scalable method for building energy efficiency assessments, which can support decisions for energy-efficient building retrofits and promote sustainable development in the construction industry. However, this study still has some limitations, such as errors in matching building features and potential changes in the building’s appearance over time (e.g., renovations). These changes may affect the accuracy of predictions.
