Classifying building energy efficiency from street view and aerial images in Denmark
Kevin Mayer kdmayer@stanford.edu Stanford University Stanford, California, USA
Gregor Heilborn gheilborn@stanford.edu Stanford University Stanford, California, USA
Martin Fischer fischer@stanford.edu Stanford University Stanford, California, USA
ABSTRACT
Assessing a building’s energy efficiency is a slow and costly process, as current methods generally require on-site visits of qualified experts. To prioritize buildings in terms of retrofit potential at scale, we propose to classify the energy efficiency of existing buildings from widely available street view and aerial imagery. Based on images for more than 21,000 buildings in Copenhagen’s metropolitan area, this case study evaluates the predictive performance of multiple end-to-end machine learning models to classify buildings as energy efficient (EU rating A-D) or inefficient (EU rating E-G). In addition, we conduct an ablation study to examine the predictive signal of each data source individually. We find that the best end-to-end machine learning model trained on street view and aerial imagery achieves a macro-averaged F1-score of 58.79% and performs on a similar level as models from a case study in the UK. This suggests that the signal in street view and aerial imagery is consistent across geographies and can therefore support large-scale analyses in terms of building energy efficiency.

To bridge this gap, we propose to estimate a building’s energy efficiency by combining widely available remotely sensed data sources, such as street view and aerial imagery in an end-to-end machine learning model. All data-driven algorithms are trained in a supervised fashion. The ground truth energy efficiency labels for each building were obtained from the Danish Energy Agency [3] which publishes the results of in-person energy audits in Denmark.
2 RELATED WORK
Building upon the wide availability of remotely sensed data sources, a growing stream of research is using data-driven algorithms to analyze and understand our natural and man-made environment at unprecedented scale [1, 14]. In terms of buildings, multiple studies have incorporated remotely sensed data sources to predict energy consumption [15, 18], photovoltaic generation [11, 13], building age [20], or property values [2]. However, only few studies combine remotely sensed data sources and data-driven algorithms to understand and analyze buildings in terms of their energy efficiency. While [16] applies quantile regression to benchmark buildings in terms of their energy efficiency, [9] estimates the energy use of 1.1 million buildings in New York by fitting linear regression, random forest, and support vector machine (SVM) algorithms. Achieving a mean absolute log accuracy ratio of 0.17, [9] identifies building age as a significant predictor in terms of energy use. In contrast to [9, 16] which rely on tabular data exclusively, [19] extends the datadriven approach by combining tabular data obtained from on-site energy audits with street view imagery. Conducting a case study for the city of Glasgow, [19] classifies more than 30,000 buildings in terms of their EU efficiency rating on a scale from A-G and achieves an accuracy of 86.8%. [10] extends this line of work by predicting building energy efficiency from remotely sensed data sources only. Conducting a case study for four regions in England, [10] performs a binary classification of buildings in terms of energy efficiency by distinguishing between buildings with EU efficiency ratings A-D and E-G from street view, aerial view, and Landsat-8-based thermal infrared data.

Only few studies incorporate information derived from remotely sensed data sources. 2.1 Dataset and Features
The dataset for this study consists of 21,593 Danish buildings from Copenhagen’s metropolitan area. Each building is represented by an aerial image, a street view image, and an associated ground truth energy performance label which specifies the building’s energy efficiency in terms of seven classes ranging from “A", the best, to “G", the worst. The ground truth energy efficiency labels have been obtained from [3] and are grouped in a binary fashion into efficient (A-D) and inefficient (E-G) labels. Based on the binary grouping, the dataset exhibits a strong class imbalance with 76.15% of the buildings falling into the energy efficient group and 23.85% into the inefficient one. Figure 1 visualizes each building in our dataset as a blue point on a map. To ensure the generalizability of our subsequent experiments, we select the training and test datasets from two distinct geographic regions within Copenhagen’s metropolitan area, which are separated by the E47 highway and the Frederikssund motorway. The test set area is marked by the polygon in Figure 1 and consists of 2,729 buildings. Of the 18,864 buildings outside of the polygon, 16,980 (90%) are used for training and 1,884 (10%) are used for validation, as illustrated in Table 1.

2.2 Aerial and Street View Imagery
The aerial and street view images are obtained from [4]. For each building the coordinates provided by [3] specify the location for which we download the imagery. The street view images are downloaded with a field of view equal to 70, a pitch of 0, and an image size of 640 × 640. The aerial images were downloaded with a zoom level of 20 and an image size of 640 × 640.
2.3 Data Cleaning and Preprocessing
The final dataset is the result of a cleaning procedure in which buildings with uninformative or unavailable images are removed. By uninformative we mean images in which the respective building
is hidden behind a fence, wall, or vegetation. To do so efficiently, we embedded each image with a pre-trained CLIP model, i.e., [12] for aerial and [5] for street view images, and cluster the obtained image embeddings with k-means clustering (k = 12). After clustering the embeddings, we visualize the corresponding images in each cluster and visually determine which clusters should be removed from the dataset. After removing uninformative clusters, we manually go through all removed images and add back informative images. Conducting this procedure separately for each data source until all clusters appear to be informative, a total of 21,089 buildings were removed from our preliminary dataset of 42,682 buildings over the course of five iterations.

3 METHODS
In terms of network architecture, the proposed end-to-end machine learning model can be split into two parts. The first part consists of two distinct CLIP-based embedding models which encode each street view and aerial image into a latent space. The second part consists of a relatively simple prediction head which classifies the image-based embeddings into efficient and inefficient properties. To obtain the image embeddings, we rely on pre-trained foundation models. For the street view images, we use StreetCLIP, a CLIP-based network which has been fine-tuned on 1.1 million geotagged images with the goal of classifying images in terms of their geolocation [5]. This model takes street view images of size 336×336 as inputs and outputs a 1024-dimensional embedding. We chose StreetCLIP, because we think that a model which has a spatial understanding of our world is also able to generate highly relevant insights for our downstream task. In terms of the aerial images, we use OpenAI’s ViT-L/14-based CLIP architecture as an image encoder which has been trained on more than 400 million image-text pairs with a contrastive loss [12]. The model takes aerial images of size 224 × 224 as inputs and outputs a 1024-dimensional embedding. Based on the 2048-dimensional concatenation of these two 1024dimensional image embeddings, we use [17] to train six different model types for our binary classification task, so-called prediction heads. First, we implement a majority model, i.e., a dumb classifier which always predicts the majority class and does not pay attention to the inputs. Second, we train a k-NN model which simply memorizes the training dataset and predicts a building’s energy efficiency label from the k nearest training embeddings. Third, we use an SVM model which can be derived via the following dual optimization problem:

Based on this formulation of the dual problem we can apply the kernel trick to make the computation more efficient and find a decision boundary which maximizes the margin between the two classes. In contrast, logistic regression approaches the classification by modeling the probability of an observation belonging to class 1 via P (y = 1|x) = 1/(1 + exp(−θT x)) and proceeds by finding the θ which minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the data via gradient descent. The fully-connected neural network (MLP) contains one hidden layer with eight ReLU-activated neurons and is trained with an adam optimizer [8]. Lastly, the gradient-boosted decision tree classifier takes inspiration from Microsoft’s LightGBM algorithm [7] and is published as HistGradientBoostingClassifier [17]. Except for logistic regression and the majority model, all prediction head algorithms are capable of finding highly non-linear decision boundaries. To account for the class imbalance in our dataset, all algorithms other than the majority model and k-NN use class weights that are inversely proportional to the class frequencies in the training dataset during training.

Due to the highly imbalanced nature of our dataset, we evaluate the performance of our models in terms of macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1 scores. This means that we compute the individual class scores in terms of precision (Equation 2), recall (Equation 3), and F1 (Equation 4) scores, before averaging the results for each metric across both classes. Since macro-averaging ensures that each class receives equal weight, this approach is well-suited to evaluate the performance of the classification algorithms on our imbalanced dataset.

4.2 Hyperparameter Search
To determine the best performing hyperparameters for each prediction head, we train each prediction head algorithm with multiple different hyperparameter settings via grid search. We then select the best performing hyperparameters on the validation set to evaluate the test set. To identify the best k-NN hyperparameter, we train nine different models where k takes on values in the range from one to nine. We find that k = 3 performs best. 4.3 Model Comparison
Table 2 displays the test set results of all six classification algorithms, sorted in ascending order in terms of F1 score. The majority model serves as our baseline classifier and achieves macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1 scores of 39.39%, 50.00%, and 43.82%, respectively. Since all other classifiers achieve higher precision, recall, and F1 scores, we can conclude that each of the five data-driven classification algorithms is able to learn meaningful patterns from the image embeddings. However, the degree to which the data-driven algorithms can learn from the embeddings varies. As an example, the k-NN-based classifier performs worst among all data-driven algorithms and achieves macro-averaged scores between 53.21-54.89% across all three metrics. In contrast, the best performing data-driven classifier, logistic regression, achieves macro-averaged scores between 58.79-61.63%, a delta of 3.9 to 8.4 percentage points across each metric. While no classifier is able to achieve macro-averaged scores beyond 62%, it is interesting to note that logistic regression performs best among all data-driven algorithms. This indicates that the algorithms finding a non-linear decision boundary, i.e., k-NN, the gradient-boosted decision tree, as well as the two-layer MLP, tend to overfit the data which hampers their generalization performance. In contrast, drawing a linear decision boundary by maximizing the margin between both classes with a linear kernel SVM also results in a subpar decision boundary when compared to logistic regression.

5 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
We presented a modified approach to estimate building energy efficiency from remotely sensed data sources by building upon [10]. To do so, we developed an end-to-end machine learning model, benchmarked six distinct prediction head classifiers, and explored the predictive power of each data source in an ablation study. We found that a logistic regression-based prediction head trained on street view embeddings performs best, achieving a macro-averaged F1 score of 61.07% and performing at a comparable level as models in [10]. In a qualitative study, we found that the algorithm struggles to classify images when the respective building is not clearly visible. While this study suggests that the predictive signal in street view and aerial images is consistent across geographies, in particular between Denmark (this study) and the UK [10], it is also clear that street view and aerial imagery alone are not sufficient to classify buildings in terms of their energy efficiency reliably. An interesting opportunity for future work could be to integrate high-resolution thermal infrared data.
